# Story arc — Indic variant

**Written:** 2026-08-12 · **Parallel to:** [`STORY_ARC.md`](STORY_ARC.md)

## What this is

The same story, the same beats, the same eight-chapter shape — renamed and rethemed around
concepts drawn from Indian philosophical and mythological traditions.

**This is a variant, not a replacement.** `STORY_ARC.md` remains authoritative until somebody
decides otherwise. Nothing here changes plot, structure, mechanics or milestones. What it
changes is what things are called and, more usefully, *what the argument is about* — because
several parts of this story map onto real debates in Indian thought far more precisely than
onto their current Western-fantasy dress.

### A note on source material

This treats concepts from Hindu, Jain and Buddhist thought as source material for a fantasy
setting, in the way the existing bible treats medieval European material. It is not a
depiction of any living religion.

Two rules followed throughout: **no deity names are used for characters**, and the
philosophical positions the factions hold are *real positions from genuine debates* rather
than invented ones. Where a faction has an argument, that argument has been made by actual
people. That is the point — it is what makes them harder to dismiss than a fantasy faction
usually is.

Names are kept short and pronounceable. Nothing longer than three syllables, no retroflex or
aspirated clusters that an English speaker would stumble over.

## Why this fits better than it has any right to

The current story's central horror is that a king burns prisoners' souls to power a city. In a
broadly Christian-derived frame that is murder plus desecration — bad, but the soul has already
gone somewhere.

**In a frame where souls transmigrate, binding one into a stone is a categorically worse
crime.** It does not merely end a life. It *stops the cycle*. A bound soul cannot be reborn,
cannot work off what it owes, cannot go on. It is held.

That single shift makes every argument in the story sharper:

- The abolitionists are not squeamish; they are objecting to the interruption of rebirth
  itself, which is the gravest thing they can name.
- The King's defence — *they were condemned anyway* — collapses on their terms and holds
  perfectly on his, because he does not believe what they believe.
- The fissures venting what was spent is no longer a horror-movie conceit. It is what happens
  when a very large number of souls are prevented from moving on and eventually stop waiting.

The existing story arrived at all of this on its own. The Indic frame simply supplies the
vocabulary it was reaching for.

### The two-term resource, which is an improvement

The current game has one word, "soul crystal," doing two jobs. The variant splits it:

- A **jiva stone** holds a soul.
- Burning it releases **prana**, which is what magic actually spends.

So the player's bar is prana and their consumable is jiva. The mechanic is unchanged; the
naming now states plainly what the transaction is. Every point of prana was somebody's jiva.

## The moral line the whole arc rests on

The existing bible identifies one thing Chapter 01 must establish or the eight-chapter argument
collapses: **organic sourcing is normal and legal**, and only the shortcut is monstrous.

The Indic frame gives that distinction a name, and it is a sharper one:

| | Term | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| Lawful sourcing | **dāna** | a gift. A jiva offered at a natural death, or taken from creatures whose turn of the wheel is short. Freely given, and giving is a virtue |
| The King's operation | **steya** | theft. A jiva taken from someone who did not offer it |

*Dāna* and *steya* — giving and stealing — are both classical categories. The trade is not
evil; **theft is**. That is the entire distinction the story needs, and it is the difference
between a defensible civilisation and a crime.

It also sharpens the King. Vikram is not defending theft in the abstract. He is arguing
**āpad-dharma** — that duty bends under emergency, that a ruler whose city is starving may do
what a ruler at peace may not. That is a real doctrine, argued at length in the Mahabharata,
and it is a much better speech than "the economy needs it."

## The Stambha — the truth

**Spoiler section. Nothing in Chapters 01–04 may state any of this.**

Everyone believes the Stambha is where jiva stones come from. It is not. It is an instrument.

It **measures** how much prana has been drawn out of the world. It **warns** when the draw
passes what the world can carry — the Chapter 01 pulse was that warning, firing for the first
time in memory. And it **holds**: it has been keeping the fissures shut for a very long time.

The pillar that keeps heaven and earth apart is old Vedic furniture — the Skambha of the
Atharvaveda is asked, in a famous hymn, what it is that holds everything up. This setting
answers: a thing somebody built, that is still working, that nobody now understands.

The inversion is unchanged:

| Faction plan | What they expect | What actually happens |
|---|---|---|
| Force it with high working | breach the source, end the shortage | the hold fails and releases everything it has been carrying |
| Break it with engines | the same, cruder | the same, faster |
| End the trade entirely | irrelevant to them — they are not trying to open it | the draw falls and the Stambha quiets on its own |

### Pretas

A **preta** is one of the departed who has not moved on — in Hindu and Buddhist cosmology, a
spirit caught between, usually depicted as hungry and unable to be satisfied.

That is exactly what a burned jiva becomes. Not destroyed: *stranded*. And when the fissures
open, what comes through is every soul the world has spent, coming back wrong.

This makes the "humans with purple fume" bestiary decision thematically inevitable rather than
a budget compromise. They look human because they were, and because nothing let them stop
being.

## Factions, and what each actually believes

The governing rule from the existing bible holds: **every faction gets a blind spot, not a
flaw.** What changes is that each blind spot is now a documented position somebody has
genuinely argued.

### Marukot — *"You cannot preach ahimsa to the starving."*

**Holds:** *āpad-dharma*. Duty in ordinary times and duty in an emergency are not the same
duty, and it is the comfortable who insist otherwise.

Marukot is desert. Jiva stones there are not luxury — they are water drawn clean, food kept
cold, the difference between a settlement and a ruin. Uttara can afford restraint because
Uttara has rain.

**Voice:** tired rather than fanatical. They have made this argument before and lost it.

**Blind spot:** they have stopped asking whether the Stambha *can* be opened, because they
cannot afford the answer to be no.

**The scene that sells them** is unchanged and still the best one available: an official walks
the Rajdoot through a settlement that went dark when Ratnapur's shipments stopped, says
nothing, and lets them do the arithmetic on their own proudest act.

### The Siddha Order — *"Name the year we should have stopped."*

**Holds:** that attainment is its own justification. Every working that now saves lives was
once an extravagance somebody wanted forbidden.

**Blind spot:** the tradition they come from *warns about them specifically.* Patanjali's Yoga
Sutras treat siddhis — attainments, powers — as real, and as obstacles: things acquired on the
way that become the reason people stop walking. The Siddha Order has spent four centuries
measuring what prana can do and never once measured what it costs, because nobody ever asked
them to and they have no instrument that would answer.

**Voice:** generous, benevolent, genuinely likeable. **The most dangerous faction should be
the warmest one.** Acharya Meera should be somebody the player is sorry about.

### Shantipur — *"We are not asking you to become poor. We are telling you what you already
are."*

**Holds:** *ahimsa paramo dharma* — non-violence is the highest duty. A power that requires a
jiva is illegitimate however politely it is taken, and consent under need is not consent. When
a poor family can offer a dying grandmother's jiva, that is not a gift; it is a market in the
dying. And the demand curve guarantees the market reaches prisoners eventually. Vikram did not
corrupt the system. He completed it.

**Blind spot — and it is a pointed one:** radical non-violence has always been most
practicable for those whose living does not require killing. Shantipur is highland, watered
and thinly peopled. **They have mistaken their geography for their virtue** and genuinely
cannot tell the difference. When Marukot says so, they have no answer — not because they are
beaten, but because it has never occurred to them.

**How to write them:** visibly poorer, dimmer, colder, and entirely unbothered. The player
should leave uncomfortable that it works.

### The Sabha — the arena

**Holds:** *rajadharma* — that rule is itself a duty with rules, and that nothing holds unless
everyone has agreed to it. Assemblies of this kind are old; *sabha* and *samiti* appear in the
Vedas as bodies a king answers to.

**Blind spot:** they will take a bad agreement over none, every time.

**How to write them:** not as comedy. The Sabha is **slow because it is real**, and its
slowness kills people. That is a far more interesting accusation than incompetence.

### The Dhruva Order — *"We did not keep it from you because you are children."*

**Holds:** that some knowledge is dangerous in proportion to how clever its holder is, and
that the Stambha's nature is the clearest case. They have kept it for generations on the
reasoning that a world which knew the pillar responds to draw would immediately try to game the
pillar. On the evidence of every other faction here, they are right.

Restricted transmission — knowledge passed only to those prepared for it — is a real and
serious tradition rather than a fantasy conceit.

**Blind spot:** their secrecy is why nobody built a second Stambha, and why nobody alive can
read the first one properly. They protected it so well they made the world unable to help
them.

### Ratnapur, and Yuvraj Arun

Arun holds no fixed position. **The Rajdoot's choices shape his**, and reading him is how the
player sees which ending they are walking toward. Unchanged from the existing bible, and the
mechanism is the same.

## The three endings are a classical argument

This is the part that fits almost uncannily well.

The Bhagavad Gita opens with a man who does not want to act because acting will cause terrible
harm, and spends its length on whether withdrawal is available to him. That is the question
this game's ending asks.

| Ending | Position | The argument against it |
|---|---|---|
| **Abolition** | *nivritti* — withdrawal from action | Withdrawal is itself a choice with consequences. Shantipur can afford it; the Maru cannot. And the debt already owed is not repaid by ceasing to add to it |
| **Regulated quota** | *pravritti* within dharma — act, but within right limit | The pie is now fixed, so somebody must be told no, and the Rajdoot decides who. Duty without the comfort of clean hands |
| **Unrestricted** | *pravritti* unchecked — artha and kama without dharma | A wager that an answer arrives before the threshold, made by people who have stopped looking for one |

The middle path is the Gita's own answer, which is a problem the existing bible already
identified: an ending that is *philosophically endorsed* is not much of a choice.

The fix stands and is now better grounded. Quota converts abundance into **allocation**, and
allocation is where dharma stops being comfortable. Somebody starves. The Rajdoot chooses. The
Gita is not a book about how doing your duty feels good — it is explicit that right action is
often the one you least want, and that is exactly the note the quota ending needs to end on.

| **prana** | life-breath, vital energy | what burning a stone releases; the player's resource |
| **preta** | one who could not move on | the fissure creatures |

| **steya** | theft | the King's operation |
| **ahimsa** | non-violence | Shantipur's founding claim |
| **āpad-dharma** | duty under distress | Marukot's defence, and the King's |

| **nivritti / pravritti** | withdrawal from action / engagement with it | the ending axis |

## What adopting this would actually cost

Cheaper than it looks, because of discipline already in place.

**Free.** Route ids, flag ids, evidence ids, skill ids, cast role ids, anchor ids and ancestry
ids are all setting-neutral already, and `WorldLayoutTests` plus `EstmereRegionTests` enforce
it. Every one of these is a display-string swap with no code change.

**Cheap.** Display names in `EstmereRegion.Anchors`, `GreyThreadSceneCatalog` titles, the
dialogue topic assets, and the fourteen authored topic responses. Text edits.

**Not free — and this is a real finding.** Scene *ids* violate the naming policy:

```
scene.estmere_palace   scene.estmere_prison   scene.caldemar_arrival   ...
```

The policy in `plan.md` says save-persisted identifiers are setting-neutral and gives
`city_west` as the model. These embed display names, so renaming the setting orphans any save
that stores a scene id — which `SaveGameV4` does, via `SaveData.SceneId`.

**This is worth fixing whether or not this variant is ever adopted.** The right form is
`scene.palace`, `scene.prison`, `scene.council_arrival` — the building, not the city. Scene
*file* names can stay as they are; they are assets, not saved state.

## What I would not do

**Do not use deity names for characters.** House Surya is a dynastic claim real kings made;
naming a character Shiva or Kali is a different act entirely, and a worse one.

**Do not make the abolitionists Jains, or the Sabha Brahmins.** The factions hold positions
drawn from these traditions; they are not those communities. The moment a faction maps onto a
real living group, every criticism the story makes of that faction lands somewhere it was not
aimed.

**Do not translate the mechanics.** Prana as a resource works because it is descriptive. Adding
chakras, or a karma meter, or reincarnating the player would be reaching for the aesthetic and
losing the argument — and the argument is the reason this variant is worth anything.
