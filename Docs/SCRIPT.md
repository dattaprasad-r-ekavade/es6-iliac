# Ratna Bay — The Script

**Status:** proposed, and the place to argue before anything is written into content files.
[`STORY.md`](STORY.md) is the structure. [`STORY_AND_SYSTEMS.md`](STORY_AND_SYSTEMS.md) is where
it meets the mechanics. **This is the plot** — what is true, who wants what, and what is
actually said.

---

## 1. The premise: an empire that ran on it before it understood it

Jiva stones are not a curiosity in a mining town. **They are what the empire is made of.**

Lamps burn them. Foundries are heated by them. Blades are quenched with them and hold an edge
no ordinary steel holds. Physicians spend them. Messages move on them. An army that carries them
beats an army that does not, and every campaign of the last two centuries was decided by who
had more.

**None of it was regulated**, because nothing that works that well ever is. Nobody asked what a
seam is for, or what happens when it is empty, or what the prana was doing before it was
convenient. There was no reason to ask: the stones came up, the lamps stayed lit, and the empire
grew on it for two hundred years.

The bill is arriving now.

### What that looks like from the bottom

The premise only matters if it is visible in how people live, so it should be visible in three
places and stated in none:

- **The army cannot fight without them.** A generation of officers has never commanded a battle
  that was not decided by prana. That is why the quota rises and why nobody in the capital will
  hear a word about slowing down.
- **The rich hoard them like bullion.** Stones are stored, not spent — a strongbox of them is
  what a great house *is*. Prices are set by people who never light one.
- **The poor fight over what is left.** A stone is a winter. A stone is a debt cleared. In the
  lower town they are counted in ones, and stolen, and killed for.

### And the hoards are leaking

This falls out of [`STORY_AND_SYSTEMS.md`](STORY_AND_SYSTEMS.md) §3 and is worth stating as
setting rather than as mechanics, because it is the sharpest image the premise produces:

> **A stockpiled stone leaks. So the wealthy quarter is the most unstable ground in Ratna Bay.**

The great houses are sitting on strongboxes bleeding prana into their own cellars. They are not
only hoarding the future — they are where it is arriving first. Nobody in those houses will say
so, and everybody in them has stopped sleeping well.

### Which makes the Deepankar a job people take out of need

This changes what the order is, and it is the change that makes the player a person rather than
a class.

**Nobody joins the Dipadhara for glory.** The work is lethal, everybody knows it is lethal, and
it is also one of the only ways in the province for somebody with nothing to end a year with
something. They join for a debt, for a family, for a place in a queue, for a winter.

That is why there is always another one to take the lamp. **The succession mechanic is a social
fact before it is a game rule** — the order never runs out of people because the province never
runs out of desperation.

It also splits the order in two, which is where the drama is:

- **The old order** — Sona, the hall, the lamps, the names on the wall. Institutional memory,
  and three centuries of shame.
- **The new intake** — most Deepankars, who arrive knowing nothing about any of it and do not
  live long enough to be told.

**The history is not hidden. It is simply not survived long enough to be inherited.** Sona is
keeping a memory for people who keep dying before they can receive it, and that is why she is so
glad when somebody finally comes back a fourth time.

### Ratna Bay is the oldest seam

Not the only one — the empire has many. **This is the first, and the deepest, and the most
worked.**

Which means whatever is going to happen everywhere is happening here first. The province is not
special; it is early. That gives the inspector a real reason to care about a distant mining town,
it makes the ending a **precedent** rather than a local decision, and it means the argument the
player settles is one the whole empire is about to have.

## 2. The keystone: what the first Deepankar found

Everything hangs off one fact, and until now it was a blank. `gov.2` says she went down to close
the seam and something stopped her. Nothing stopped her.

> **She closed it. It worked. Ratna Bay starved.**
>
> The lamps went out, the foundries stopped, the physicians had nothing, and in one season the
> province lost more people than the mines had taken in twenty years. So the order went back
> down and **opened it again** — and then rewrote what it was for.

Every Deepankar since has been maintaining the thing the order was founded to end. **They know.**
It is why the hall is evasive, why the shrine is uncomfortable, why nobody says the old name out
loud. The order is not corrupt. It is ashamed, and it is right to be, and it is not obvious that
it was wrong.

This does four things no other version of the answer does:

- It makes the governor's line **literally true** rather than an excuse: *"the alternative is a
  province that starves in a season."* He has the precedent.
- It explains **capture without a villain.** Nobody had to be bought. They saw what closing it
  cost and could not do it twice.
- It gives **The Release** a real price. The player would be repeating a documented catastrophe
  on purpose, with the numbers in front of them.
- It puts the moral argument **in the past tense.** The question is not *should someone do the
  right thing* — someone did, and this is what happened.

### And she is still down there

Not as a body. **Uttara never rose and never left.** Whatever holds the seam from becoming a
door is her, still doing the job, three hundred years past the point of it meaning anything.

She is the fight at the bottom, and she is not an enemy. She is the last Deepankar still working.

## 3. Why the supply is failing *now*

The middle act needs a cause or it is just errands. It is political, not geological:

> **The capital raised the quota.**

Mines fill the treasury; the treasury pays the army; the army cannot fight without stones. An
empire preparing for something wants more, and the province cannot meet it from the lawful supply
— the almost-dead do not die faster to order. So the difference comes out of the living, and it
has been growing for nine years, which is what `clerk.1` already says.

**And this time the inspection arrives.** `barracks.2` already establishes that a mahamatra comes
every five years and *"the last one did not reach us."* This one does.

---

## 4. Who the protagonist is

**The order.** Not the player character, who dies repeatedly by design.

Individual Deepankars get a **voice** — one line about who they were, and per §1 that line is
almost always about money. The **arc** belongs to the Dipadhara: founded to end this, failed by
succeeding, three centuries of maintaining it, handed one more chance by somebody who keeps
coming back up.

Practically: nobody in the fort ever says *"you"* about the founding. They say *"we."*

---

## 5. What the preta want

They are beings of unsatisfiable want, and it turns out the want is the plot.

> **To be counted.**

*"Forty of us went in on the governor's order. The register says twelve."*

The mountain is full of people the state did not write down. That is why they cannot rest and why
prana holds them: they are owed a number. It makes the humblest room in the fort — a table, a
ledger, a tally-keeper — the room an ending turns on.

**And Uttara is uncounted too.** The register lists the office, not the name. The founder
suffered the same erasure as the convict gangs she went down to answer for, and nobody noticed
because nobody reads the old rolls.

## 6. The cast, and where the pressure comes from

There is **no villain**, and that should be defended rather than fixed. There is a quota, and
everybody is somewhere between complicit and trapped.

| Who | Wants | Is trapped by |
|---|---|---|
| **Tissa**, tally-keeper | The books to balance | Not being permitted to say they do not |
| **Sona**, lamp-keeper | Somebody to live long enough to be told | An order that dies before it inherits |
| **Visakha**, physician | To stop buying what she buys | People who die without it |
| **Suvarnapala**, registrar | The quota met | The capital, who set it |
| **Isidata**, priest | The dead pitied | Being tolerated only while he stays quiet |
| **Chandrashri**, clerk | The true figures read | Three replies telling her to check her arithmetic |
| **Vasumitra**, governor | To hand it to somebody else | Thirty years of choosing none of the options |
| **The mahamatra** | The province audited | Nothing. He is the only free person in it |

**The inspector is the antagonist and is not evil.** He is doing his job correctly, which is what
makes him frightening: no argument works on him, because he is not wrong about anything he is
actually claiming.

## 7. The beat sheet

### Act I — The job (runs 1–8, Yukta to Sthanika)

The economy, learned by doing. The fort is warm, the work is honest, and the only strange thing
is that the ledger never balances and the tally-keeper will not discuss it.

**Already written:** `gate.1`, `hall.1`, `assay.1`, `forge.1`.

**Needed:** the first preta testimony, in a mine, on a kill — the first crack, and it should
arrive before any fort room admits anything.

> **Turn I** — *the supply does not add up.* `phys.1`. The order sends you to find out why the
> price is rising.

### Act II — The complicity (runs 8–25, Pradeshika to Adhyaksha)

Every step is a conversation with a decision:

1. **The trader.** Argue the price down. What you offer costs the order something.
2. **The shortage.** Explain it — to the registrar, or the physician. What you say is recorded.
3. **The armourer.** The order wants supplies the province will not release, because the army
   has first call on everything.
4. **The inspection is announced.** The books do not balance.
5. **The fort falls.** Rooms are held. Instability arrives as plot rather than weather.
6. **The questions.** You start asking the clerk, then the governor, what nobody asks.

> **Turn II** — *the first Deepankar.* `gov.2`. Her name, and that she went down to close it.
> The governor does not tell you she succeeded, because he does not know.

### Act III — The seam, and the choice

**The seam.** One authored descent. You find Uttara, learn that it worked, and learn what it
cost. The only hand-authored level in the game, and this is why it earns it.

**The fort falls properly.** What happens at the seam destabilises everything. The finale is the
fort overrun, cleared room by room against a clock, and each room you reach is one more person
telling you what they would do.

**The choice**, with all three prices known.

## 8. The three endings

The premise raises the stakes on all three, and it turns The Lamp from villainy into a position.

| | What you do | What happens | Who pays |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The Ledger** | Write the true count. Every name, including Uttara's | The dead are counted and rest. The corrected books reach the capital, and the whole arrangement is finally looked at | Ratna Bay, which is finished as a place. And it will be looked at everywhere else too |
| **The Lamp** | Take the trade over and impose the regulation nobody ever imposed | The province lives. The supply is measured, rationed, and permanent. Somebody is finally in charge | Whoever is being drained, forever and efficiently. You are the thing the order was founded to stop, and you are good at it |
| **The Release** | Close the seam, as she did | It works, exactly as it worked before. The oldest seam in the empire goes quiet, and everything built on it starts to fail | Everyone, in one season, with the precedent in front of you |

**None is clean, and the game never says which is right.** The Ledger is what the preta are
asking for and it ends the town. The Lamp saves the most lives and makes you the villain of the
next three hundred years. The Release is the honest one and it is a catastrophe somebody already
ran the experiment on.

**Default: The Lamp.** A player who committed to nothing gets the ending for people who commit to
nothing, and the province goes on as it was. That is not a punishment; it is the truest outcome
of indifference, and it should be delivered without comment.

## 9. Rules for the writing itself

**The premise lives in objects, never in speeches.** A lamp with a stone in it. A blade that
holds an edge it should not. A strongbox in a house the player cannot enter. Beggars at the gate
counting in ones. If a character explains the economy, the scene has failed — the player should
work it out from what is lit and who is warm.

**Nobody explains the setting.** No character has ever had to be told what a jiva stone is. A
line that exists only to inform the player is cut, and the world stays confusing for another hour.

**Two registers, held consistently.** Officials speak in Sanskrit forms and bureaucratic nouns;
everybody else in Prakrit forms and short sentences. An official who slips into the common
register is being kind; a local who uses the formal one is being sarcastic.

**Nobody argues the theme.** No speeches about greed. The verse on the pillar is the only place
the argument is stated outright, and it was carved by the state, in a mine the state opened, to
take wealth out of it. Adding a character who notices that out loud ruins it.

**One idea per fragment, two lines at most.** They are read between runs by somebody with a door
waiting.

**The order says "we" about the founding.** Always. Three hundred years old, and inherited.

**Preta testimony is a number and a name, never a plea.** *"Forty went in. The register says
twelve."* Not *"help us."* The horror is the arithmetic.

**A successor's line is almost always about money.** They did not come for glory. One clause,
and never self-pitying.

## 10. Still open

- **What the empire is arming for.** The quota rose for a reason. It probably never needs to be
  said, and nobody in the province knowing is better than any answer.
- **Whether the player ever sees the rich quarter.** The strongest version is a place you can see
  and never enter — but a haunted wealthy district is also the most interesting level in the
  premise, and it is currently nowhere in the plan.
- **Does the player ever learn the order reopened the seam?** The darkest fact in the game.
  Suggested: from Sona, at the highest rank, in the hall — the only person who would say it, and
  only to somebody who has come back often enough to be worth telling.
- **Whether the mahamatra can be turned.** Currently no, and that is stronger. Worth one pass to
  be sure the game is not refusing something a player will reasonably expect.
- **Names** for the province, the empire, and the governor's house.
- **Uttara's second name** — the one the order kept and the register never had.
